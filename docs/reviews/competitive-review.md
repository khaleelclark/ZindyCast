# ZindyCast: independent competitive review

Reviewed September 11, 2026, approximately 02:37–02:43 UTC. Independent product review, with no implementation ownership or changes.

**Verdict: competitive only in a narrow private-family niche, as a heat-aware companion. It is not yet a competitive general weather replacement.** Its useful distinction is a transparent, hourly estimated outdoor WBGT alongside feels-like weather, without advertisements or an account requirement in the tested flows. That distinction is real, but the current mobile hierarchy, incomplete forecast/map experience, and technical history workflows prevent it from replacing Weather Underground, Weather.com, AccuWeather, or Windy for ordinary daily use. Even the niche needs a shorter path from numbers to planning.

The previous blanket description that the map “doesn't work” does **not** describe my test: radar rendered precipitation near Austin, satellite rendered, and manual frame selection and zoom worked. Rendering is now ahead of the map's consumer usability.

## What actually works, and what falls short

**Observed — useful strengths.** Austin search distinguished county/state and coordinates among ambiguous results. Saving it produced a quick-access place; it survived reload. Switching Fahrenheit/Celsius converted displayed heat, wind and rain units. Selecting strenuous activity changed the educational advice without changing environmental WBGT. The default daily view showed seven rows; 14-day and 10-day selections exposed the longer series. The 48-hour plots and expandable tables provide complementary access. Source text distinguishes modeled conditions, official NWS alerts and observed imagery. No active-alert text was clearly attributed; this was not a test of an active severe warning.

**Observed — mobile priorities are inverted.** In the saved-Austin, 10-day view at 390×844, headings were at approximately y=838 for feels-like, y=1177 for WBGT, y=2075 for the local map, y=3580 for hourly weather and y=4278 for daily outlook. These are document coordinates measured in the browser, not usability scores. Header/search/saved-place rows and a large empty-alert card occupy almost the entire first screen. The desktop two-column arrangement looks more deliberate, but the tall map/heat row still pushes everyday forecast planning well below the fold. [Desktop](competitive/today-desktop.png), [mobile](competitive/today-mobile.png). The latter captures a map loading transition after resize; its empty-looking radar must not be read as clear weather.

**Inference — the “too much reading” complaint still applies.** Collapsing many details helped, but repeated availability counts, interval terminology, technical selector names and caution text dominate the daily journey. The issue is information ordering, not merely an outdated component library. MUI is already recorded in the foundation and reflected in the current controls; another visual-framework migration would not fix this hierarchy.

**Observed — forecast completeness and trust details.** Current and hourly clear-sky icons showed a sun at Austin's 9 PM local hour. Sunrise/sunset was unavailable. Daily rows show ranges and rainfall totals but no compact condition narrative or daily rain probability; a day's first partial range also carries multiple counts (“rain intervals,” “hours,” “temp values”). Disclosure is preferable to false whole-day claims, but this is harder to understand than “Tonight” and “Tomorrow.” Temperature/feels-like are the primary weather plot; richer measurements require the table. No daily heat outlook or minute-by-minute rain prediction was observed. I did not establish superior or inferior forecast accuracy.

**Observed — maps now render, but remain an expert form.** Both a radar image with precipitation and longwave satellite pixels appeared; previous-frame changed the advertised selection, and zoom changed scale to 20 km. No uncaught browser page errors were recorded in the live session. However, the map exposes product terminology and UTC timestamps before the image, hides the legend in a disclosure, and requires repeated manual frame clicks. Its capability text explicitly says animation and temperature/wind overlays are forthcoming. Phone selectors truncate long product names. The standing coverage/actual-time uncertainty notice is honest but leaves a family user unable to tell confidently whether the displayed frame is sufficiently current. [Radar](competitive/maps-desktop.png), [satellite](competitive/satellite-mobile.png), [capability text](competitive/maps-expanded.txt). This sample does not validate image georegistration, coverage, Alaska/Hawaii, pan extremes or every advertised timestamp.

**Observed and inference — WBGT is the differentiator and the presentation burden.** Austin showed 78.5°F estimated outdoor WBGT at the 9 PM hourly instant, separately from ordinary wet-bulb at 76°F. Expanded information correctly names Liljegren, the FAO short-grass wind adjustment, modeled endpoint radiation, and natural wet-bulb as a separate component. Tulsa's bands are explicitly a reference, not the calculation method. The [NWS Tulsa source](https://www.weather.gov/tsa/wbgt) labels its calculator a nonoperational prototype; that distinction matters. [Expanded app evidence](competitive/heat-expanded.txt).

The green-to-red scale and “Lower/Higher heat stress” labels nevertheless invite an implicit safe/unsafe interpretation that the nearby disclaimer asks users to undo. That is a design risk, not evidence that numeric categories were silently applied. A small 78.5 value competes with a huge 91 feels-like value; activity advice sits farther away and is general. The app does not yet turn its excellent extra metric into an easy comparison of lower- versus higher-estimated-heat hours. Keep uncertainty, but give the primary view a brief explanation and relative hourly planning; retain detailed methods and raw diagnostics behind an explicitly technical disclosure. Do not invent personalized categories or exposure times.

**Observed — history is usable for an enthusiast; climate comparison is unfinished as a family workflow.** One Austin ERA5 day, July 15, 2020 UTC, loaded 24/24 complete hours, a chart and table. CSV downloaded with 24 data rows plus header, explicit SI units and source/interval fields; the first exported 40°C matches the displayed 104°F. This was a spot-check, not a full export audit. [Result](competitive/history-result-mobile.png), [CSV](competitive/history-export.csv). Export wording warns that the server regenerates data, so revisions may differ from the displayed retrieval. Users would reasonably prefer an exact export of the view.

Reanalysis offers day/month/custom up to 31 days and explicitly excludes wet-bulb/WBGT. Station mode asks for an 11-character GHCN-Daily ID and says station metadata is not retrieved; it does not help a normal user find a suitable station. Compare exposes 2–5 saved cities and 1–366 UTC days, tells the user to save another city elsewhere, and describes browser registration/storage consequences. It is not the requested 1991–2020 or latest-ten-year seasonal comparison experience. No job was submitted, so result rendering, cancellation and comparison exports remain untested. [Station entry](competitive/history-station.txt), [Compare](competitive/compare-mobile.png).

**Observed — failure handling and practical accessibility.** A browser-local API network block produced separate forecast/alert failures and a retry control, kept navigation and saved settings available, and did not substitute plausible weather on reload. The message was the technical “Failed to fetch”; the empty result would benefit from a plain-language recovery explanation. [Simulated failure](competitive/simulated-network-failure.png). Loading text was visible during map changes and later cleared. Keyboard Tab reached “Skip to weather”; Enter focused `main`. Mobile page width equaled viewport width, but the horizontal tab strip clipped end labels, and native map zoom controls are visually small. Tables offer useful non-chart access. This was not a screen-reader, contrast, full keyboard-map or touch-target audit.

**Inference — niche economics and deployment.** No ads, no account ceremony and independent family preferences can be advantages at a $0 service budget. However, private tailnet access, phone installation, independent settings and an operator-maintained server are adoption costs absent from a normal public weather link. No cross-device sync is an intentional tradeoff, not automatically a defect. [Settings](competitive/settings-mobile.png) honestly says notifications are not available. Together with the physical-device and recovery limits in the hardening report, this supports a private companion designation, not a mass-market or comprehensive family-release claim.

## Five priorities, ranked by severity and product impact

1. **High — put the daily decision in the first screen.** Compact the inactive-alert state and repeated location controls; place current/feels-like, concise estimated WBGT, upcoming hours and tomorrow before the full map and scientific details. **Acceptance:** at 390×844 with one saved city and no active warning, current/feels-like is visible without scrolling and an hourly preview begins within the first two viewports; active warnings remain prominent. At 1440×1000, current, a useful map preview and tonight/tomorrow summary are visible together. Preserve access to every disclosure.
2. **High — finish and clarify basic forecast presentation.** Correct night/day icon selection; expose meaningful tonight/tomorrow summaries and daily precipitation probability; supply validated sunrise/sunset or a useful unavailable reason. **Acceptance:** local-night samples never show a sun for clear conditions; daily partial periods are plainly named; 7/10/14 views expose consistent units, condition/rain information and the same underlying dates. Add a direct jump to Hourly/Daily instead of requiring several screens of scrolling.
3. **High — make radar useful for motion and timing.** Add bounded play/pause and scrubbing, a persistent legend and readable local-time/age context; simplify product names, with technical metadata expandable. **Acceptance:** a phone user can identify the selected layer, interpret colors, select or animate recent frames and distinguish loading/missing imagery without opening documentation. Preserve independently correct advertised/observed-time claims; do not replace an unconfirmed time with a guessed “live” label. Temperature/wind overlays remain explicitly unavailable until supported.
4. **High — make WBGT interpretable without implied safety certification.** Tie the numeric estimate to its hour and a short explanation, and make comparison of forecast hours easy. Reconsider the traffic-light visual semantics while universal categories remain unapproved. **Acceptance:** a family usability check can distinguish air temperature, feels-like, ordinary wet-bulb and modeled outdoor WBGT, identify a relatively lower-WBGT upcoming period, and explain that this is not a safe-exposure promise. Activity changes advice but not the environmental estimate; methods and source-version evidence remain reachable.
5. **Medium — complete the heat/history decision paths and declare release boundaries.** Add inline city selection for Compare and assisted station discovery with metadata, then deliver the planned multi-year seasonal products or label this explicitly as short-period comparison. Make CSV behavior predictable. **Acceptance:** a new user can choose two cities from Compare without navigating away; choose an appropriate station without knowing its ID; tell modeled from observed data; and export a clearly defined selected result. No 30-year/decade claim until those windows and their completeness are actually supported. Settings/onboarding should also make current notification and private-connectivity limitations clear before reliance.

The most credible next milestone is **a fast private weather dashboard with an unusually good heat-planning view**. That is narrower than the original complete-weather ambition, but it is a useful product position. Competing broadly requires completing the ordinary forecast and map journey, not adding more scientific text or cosmetic theming.
